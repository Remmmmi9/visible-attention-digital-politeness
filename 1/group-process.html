Visual Podcast / Making the Podcast

From an Everyday Pause to a Visual Podcast

This page traces how our visual podcast developed from a familiar messaging moment into a public-facing media project.

Our aim was not to compare whether WhatsApp or WeChat is better. We wanted to ask a more subtle question: what happens when someone’s attention becomes visible before they are ready to reply?

The making process included topic development, group planning, script writing, recording, visual design, editing, source organisation and ethics checking.

01

Finding the Topic

The project started from a small but familiar situation: a message has been seen, but the reply is not ready yet. This moment felt valuable because it is ordinary enough for many people to recognise, but also complex enough to open up questions about pressure, politeness and digital visibility.

Our group decided to focus on read visibility and reply pressure in messaging. We later named the visual podcast Ease Talks: Seen, But Not Ready.

02

Shaping the Focus

At first, this topic could easily have become a simple comparison between WhatsApp and WeChat. Later, we realised that the more important question was not which app is better, but how different platform environments make attention, delay and politeness visible in different ways.

This shift helped the project move beyond comparing platform features and focus more on the social meanings within digital communication. For me, this was especially clear. I had used WeChat for many years, but only started using WhatsApp more often after moving to the UK. Because of this, I was more sensitive to the difference between visible and less visible replying. My own experience also helped me share with the group how different digital spaces can create different kinds of pressure, rhythm and communication habits.

03

Writing the Discussion Script

We wanted the script to feel like a natural conversation, while still being guided by clear ideas. Through personal experiences, group questions and concept cards, we connected everyday examples with module concepts.

One key moment in my script was about reading a WhatsApp message but needing time to check information or think about how to say something in English. Once the message is marked as read, the silence can feel more noticeable. It may even look like I am ignoring someone, although I am actually still thinking.

This also helped us turn “reply pressure” from a private feeling into a question that could be discussed more publicly: why can one small read receipt change how people understand silence?

04

Recording and Visual Editing

We chose the visual podcast format because this topic is not only about language. The audience needs to see the interface signals we are talking about, such as blue ticks, timestamps, notification previews, typing indicators, voice messages and the pause before replying.

The visual layer made the discussion more suitable for a public audience. Screen recordings helped show interface features. Recreated chat examples helped explain the situation. Subtitles made the video easier to follow. Concept cards helped connect everyday moments with ideas such as affordances, modes and media, awareness cues and face work.

The recording process itself also taught us a lot. It was our first time using the school’s Production Room, and the equipment felt unfamiliar at the beginning. We were a little clumsy at first and spent some time figuring out how everything worked, testing the sound and checking the visuals. We also met a few small technical problems. But this process helped us realise that a public-facing project is not just about recording the content. It also needs attention to sound, image, subtitles, rhythm and whether the audience can understand the work smoothly. In the end, we solved the equipment issues and completed both the filming and post-production.

05

Sources, Ethics and Final Organisation

The final stage included organising sources, media credits and ethical notes. I helped organise the references so that the project was not based only on personal opinions, but also supported by module readings and relevant research.

At the same time, because this project deals with everyday messaging and private communication, we also needed to consider ethical issues. We tried to avoid using overly private or sensitive chat content, and used recreated chat examples and interface recordings to explain our points.
